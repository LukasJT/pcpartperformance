## M02 — DDR5-6000 CL30 vs CL36: what the 2 ns calculation can tell you

**Proposed canonical:** /blog/ram-timings-cas-trcd-trp/
**Intent:** Materially deepen the existing timing-interpretation intent with a specific 6000 MT/s CL30/CL36 decision path
**Existing intent:** growth G04
**Intent decision:** Material upgrade of growth G04; preserve the timing guide canonical rather than adding a competing CL30/CL36 article.
**Status:** Complete, AI source checked; owner release decision pending. No personal human review is recorded.
**Proposed public author:** PC Part Performance.
**Disclosure:** Research and writing prepared with AI assistance. No personal human review or hands-on testing is claimed.
**Evidence checked:** 2026-10-13; publication and update dates remain unset.
**Content/evidence SHA-256:** b15d8121ffd1641421b9346a06d568bb396d63a544d6943b703daeb818e681a4

### Answer

At the same 6000 MT/s transfer rate, CL30 corresponds to a calculated 10 ns CAS interval and CL36 to 12 ns. That 2 ns difference is useful specification arithmetic. It is not a measured gaming advantage, a full-system latency result or proof that either profile works on your motherboard.

### Make the same-rate comparison first

For conventional DDR timing arithmetic, nanoseconds per clock cycle equal 2,000 divided by the data rate in MT/s. At 6000 MT/s that is one-third of a nanosecond. Multiplying by 30 gives 10 ns; multiplying by 36 gives 12 ns. These are calculated CAS intervals, with the rate and cycle count supplied as inputs.

Keep the units beside the result. CL is a count of clock cycles, whereas MT/s describes transfers per second. Comparing CL30 at one rate with CL36 at another rate requires a new calculation. For example, a hypothetical 6400 MT/s CL32 setting also gives 10 ns. Equal calculated intervals do not make two memory configurations identical.

**Original DDR arithmetic examples, not tests of named retail kits**

| Stated specification | Formula | Calculated CAS interval |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 6000 MT/s CL30 | 30 × 2000 ÷ 6000 | 10 ns |
| 6000 MT/s CL36 | 36 × 2000 ÷ 6000 | 12 ns |
| 6400 MT/s CL32 | 32 × 2000 ÷ 6400 | 10 ns |

### Read the rest of the timing string and kit identity

CORSAIR explains that primary timing labels describe different operations: CL, tRCD, tRP and tRAS are not alternate names for one complete delay. An abbreviated listing may show only CL. Preserve the full labeled specification and the profile it belongs to instead of treating the first number as the whole memory subsystem.

Record the exact kit part number, capacity, module count and advertised profile. Kingston’s decoder illustrates that those identity fields are distinct and product-family specific. Two packages with the same capacity and visible heat spreader may still describe different configurations. Compare the actual items and installation conditions, not a generic category called “CL30 RAM.”

Source context: [CORSAIR — CAS latency and DDR5 timings explained](https://www.corsair.com/uk/en/explorer/diy-builder/memory/what-is-cas-latency-ddr5-latencies-explained/); [Kingston — Memory part-number decoder](https://www.kingston.com/en/memory/memory-part-number-decoder)

### Separate advertised and operating settings

The rate on the package is not automatically the rate currently selected in firmware. AMD describes EXPO as an overclocking profile technology. A supported feature and an advertised kit profile still need the applicable CPU, board, firmware and module-count conditions; this guide does not certify that every AM5 setup will operate at 6000 MT/s.

For an existing PC, record the active rate and timings before comparing a replacement. For a new build, verify the intended capacity and establish a working baseline before optional settings. Follow the board and memory maker’s supported instructions. Do not copy voltage changes or hidden BIOS values from a different configuration merely to make the arithmetic look attainable.

Source context: [AMD — EXPO memory profiles](https://www.amd.com/en/products/processors/technologies/expo.html)

### Do not turn 2 ns into an FPS percentage

The 2 ns difference in the first table concerns one calculated interval. It does not establish a fixed uplift in games, application responsiveness or 1% lows. Those outcomes need comparable measurements with the game or workload, CPU, exact memory configuration, graphics settings and test method recorded. Missing measurements should stay missing.

Use the performance evidence page to distinguish measured results from specifications and unvalidated models. Even a legitimate result for one game and processor must not become a blanket promise for every PC. The timing calculator can answer its mathematical question accurately without claiming to predict frames. Neither the smaller interval nor a higher purchase price earns an automatic recommendation.

### Compare the complete purchase instead of one label

Choose the needed capacity and supported physical configuration first. Then compare two exact kits in the same region and currency with actual seller and return conditions. Keep unknown prices explicit. A less expensive adequately supported kit may leave more budget for a documented need; a smaller calculated interval may matter only if applicable workload evidence justifies paying for it.

For an existing owner, include keeping the working kit. If capacity, stability and the tasks you use are satisfactory, a timing label alone does not demonstrate a reason to replace it. If the current system is unstable, use the diagnostic workflow before purchasing around an assumed performance problem. A specification comparison is only one part of a useful memory decision.

### Save the inputs and the uncertainty

Use the two-kit timing tool with rate and cycle values from the same kind of evidence in both columns. Leave unknown tRCD and tRP values blank rather than entering zero. Keep advertised settings, operating settings and measured workload results in separate columns of your notes.

The final record should identify the kits, support evidence, calculated intervals, price basis and the condition that would change your choice.

M.2 describes a physical format, not a universal promise of NVMe support. Keep the drive protocol and host-slot support in the same record. A 2.5-inch drive requires its own supported connection and mounting arrangement. Do not assume that buying an adapter creates support for an otherwise unsupported boot or storage configuration.

Record the old drive’s exact identity and whether it contains an operating system or valuable files. Make and check an appropriate backup before any operation that can erase data. ### Answer

An AM4 drop-in candidate can preserve a supported board and DDR4 kit; an AM5 route changes the platform and requires DDR5. Compare complete supported configurations, including keeping the current PC. The CPU price alone cannot establish the cost or usefulness of either upgrade.

### Define the problem the upgrade needs to solve

Write the current CPU, board revision, memory kit, GPU, cooling, PSU and storage. Then name the observed limitation: an application that takes too long, a repeatable game-performance problem, insufficient capacity or a missing feature. Keep the observation and test conditions; the age of the platform is not a diagnosis.

Include changing nothing as a route. A configuration or software issue may deserve investigation before a parts order. If you cannot demonstrate that the current platform fails a requirement, a newer socket name alone does not establish value. The upgrade planner should preserve the existing inventory and the evidence that makes a proposed replacement relevant.

### Use exact CPUs to understand the platform dependency

AMD’s live official support record identifies the Ryzen 7 5700X3D as AM4/DDR4, requiring discrete graphics and listing no included boxed thermal solution. AMD’s Ryzen 5 7600 product record identifies AM5/DDR5, integrated Radeon graphics and a Wraith Stealth solution for the listed boxed package. These are illustrative model facts, not a recommendation that one always replaces the other.

The dependency matters more than a two-CPU price subtraction. An existing DDR4 kit does not become the memory for the specified AM5 route. A shared AM4 socket still does not establish whether your exact board and installed BIOS support the 5700X3D. Check package contents and applicable board outputs instead of borrowing another seller’s photo or bundle description.

Include the cooler, mount, graphics output path and any preparation required to update firmware while the current CPU still works. If support cannot be established, do not buy around a presumed drop-in upgrade. Model-specific documentation can make a reuse route attractive, but “keep the board” is an outcome of checks rather than a shortcut around them.

The platform worksheet should include CPU, board, memory, cooling and mounting accessories, installation extras, and any other required change. Mark owned reusable parts separately from new spending. A reused case, drive or PSU still needs its own dimensional, connection and condition checks; an asset you already own is not automatically compatible.

Use actual CAD or USD quotes with a consistent scope and date. Missing prices stay visible, and taxes or delivery exclusions stay beside the total. Do not call a partial subtotal the cost of moving to AM5. This article supplies no current offer set or exact ready-to-buy bill of materials, so a price-performance verdict is intentionally unresolved.

### Require workload evidence for the performance decision

Compare original tests only when the games or applications, settings and relevant hardware conditions match your intended use. A cache size, clock or core-count difference cannot establish a universal upgrade gain. A measured result on a different GPU or rendering mode may not answer the same question.

If comparable evidence is missing, state the likely constraint as a hypothesis and preserve the uncertainty. Do not supply a bottleneck percentage or inferred FPS uplift. The specification and cost work can still be useful: it tells you what the change requires and what evidence would justify it. Keep that distinction visible when sharing the plan.

### Choose the route that solves the stated requirement

A supported AM4 change can make sense when it solves a documented need while retaining useful components. A replacement platform can make sense when its documented capabilities or supported workload evidence justify the additional purchases. Keeping the PC can make sense when neither expense addresses the observed problem. These are conditions, not universal rankings.

Save each alternative as a complete parts revision with reuse choices, quotes and remaining checks. If you will buy in stages, open the phased-upgrade guide and verify every intermediate machine. Preserve a backup and legitimate recovery access before hardware changes. The outcome should explain what is kept, what changes and why the whole expense is justified.

### Make the complete decision record before ordering

Use the worksheet to compare candidates on the same requirements. Add exact region-specific prices only when documented, including required approved cables, delivery and other exclusions. Do not conceal a missing accessory price inside a partial subtotal. A manufacturer specification, a current seller quote and a measured performance review are different evidence types.

Save the selected supply and outstanding checks with the complete build. Confirm that a later GPU, case or drive-layout change does not inherit an old verdict. The result should be a connection and installation plan someone can understand, not a claim of “100% compatible” based solely on wattage. No PSU testing, noise rating or current best-price ranking was performed for this guide.
