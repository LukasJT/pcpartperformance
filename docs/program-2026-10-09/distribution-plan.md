## Capacity and release conditions

Assume **10 hours/week**: six for a human hardware editor, three for engineering/QA, one for distribution. No staffing is currently confirmed. Review two or three researched items a week, retain time for corrections and current-source checks, and reduce the cadence if review takes longer. Forty drafts are a queue, not a promise to publish forty articles in 90 days. Never publish blocked budget BOMs, price claims or workstation recommendations to meet the schedule.

Human review is required for every new item and each material existing-page replacement. Exact product identity, offer rights, stock, currency and budget completeness are additional gates. No paid links, purchased traffic, invented testing or automatic community posting.

## Six short video scripts

These scripts describe implemented staging features. Record on production only after the specific feature is approved and released. Replace a shot if a gate remains unresolved; never imply a public feature is live merely because its draft exists.

1. **Before buying your first PC.** “Write down what the entire purchase must include, then open Build School. Its checklist covers the parts list, support documents, assembly checks and first boot. Progress stays in this browser, so export or print it. A checked box records your confirmation; it does not certify fit.” Shots: master path → check one item → export → builder.
2. **A socket match is one check.** “Load the builder’s example and open its readiness report. A documented check can pass while firmware, connectors or clearance still need verification. Open the exact support documentation next. The useful result is knowing what to confirm before ordering.” Shots: example → passed section → BIOS unknown → source.
3. **Keep what you already own.** “Choose your exact installed parts in the upgrade planner. Create a reuse plan, inspect an alternative and add a phase. The system rechecks the resulting configuration. It does not invent a performance uplift or recommend replacing everything just because it is old.” Shots: inventory → keep/verify → phase → builder.
4. **The missing-price trap.** “A partial subtotal is not the price of a complete PC. This report identifies parts that still need a current exact quote. The Budget Build Lab lets you enter both complete purchase plans, including cooling and accessories. Blank is unknown; owned and not-needed items are separate choices.” Shots: missing prices → quote fields → incomplete warning.
5. **Make your next decision easier to revisit.** “Save a named build revision locally, swap one part, then compare the selections. A share link preserves exact parts and your ownership choices. Anyone with the link can view the configuration; it is not private. Prices are checked separately when opened.” Shots: save → swap → compare revision → copy link.
6. **Why there is no universal FPS number.** “The game, version, scene, driver and graphics modes matter. Our PC tool currently has no approved matching measured dataset. It says insufficient evidence. An optional unvalidated calculation is clearly separated. Console cards show source-linked modes or targets, which do not promise sustained FPS in every scene.” Shots: default evidence result → conditions → console source.

## Three substantial video outlines

**First PC, from inventory to first boot:** define workload and full budget; read the exact manuals; load an example without calling it universally recommended; identify missing cooling/firmware/price evidence; safely assemble following the manuals; inspect cables without opening the PSU; confirm firmware recognition; use official OS/drivers; save the configuration and stock-settings baseline. Include troubleshooting paths and export the checklist.

**Upgrade one part or change the platform:** confirm existing exact IDs; record a reproducible symptom; demonstrate the do-nothing/configuration option; compare a GPU change with CPU/board/memory/cooling costs; plan phases and recheck each; compare manually supplied complete quotes; acknowledge evidence gaps rather than inventing an uplift. Finish by saving and sharing the chosen plan.

**Read a benchmark and a compatibility result correctly:** show two test descriptions with different scenes/settings; explain why they cannot be averaged into a precise result; distinguish native frames, upscaling and frame generation; demonstrate an exact documented compatibility check; enter manual dimensions; explain why a conceptual 3D mesh is not a clearance certificate; open original sources and the protocol for future site-owned tests.

## Helpful community-answer drafts

**First boot, no display:** “Record the board’s diagnostic indication before changing anything. Disconnect power before inspecting/reseating. Check the exact motherboard manual, CPU support/BIOS, required power plugs and the display output supported by the selected graphics device. Make one change at a time. A no-display symptom does not by itself justify a new GPU. A checklist can help organize those observations.” Link the released troubleshooting resource only where community rules permit and disclose that you operate the site.

**AM4 versus a new platform:** “Compare the whole purchase, not just processor prices: motherboard, memory, cooling/mounting and required accessories may change. Keep a configuration baseline and test the workload you care about. Without comparable measurements, a fixed uplift percentage is not evidence. Existing parts may still meet the goal.” Offer the complete-cost worksheet as optional context, not a compulsory promotional link.

**Will a card fit?** “Verify the exact partner-card variant, length, thickness, height and power connector position. Case clearance may change with radiators, brackets or drive cages. Manufacturer body dimensions help, but they do not prove the installed cable route. Do not substitute a similar-looking card or mesh for measurements.” Link the exact manuals before any site tool.
