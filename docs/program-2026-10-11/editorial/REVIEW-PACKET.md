Compare exact Canadian configurations, seller support and delivered CAD costs before choosing a gaming desktop.

### Start with a job and a complete system

A useful shortlist begins with the games, display and working space you already have. Write down your display resolution, desired experience and any work that shares the machine. A desktop for a living-room television has different constraints from one used beside a microphone. Decide whether you need Wi-Fi, enough front ports, a small enclosure or room for another drive. These requirements help you reject unsuitable systems before an attractive GPU name takes over the decision.

Set a total Canadian-dollar limit for the purchase. Include the computer, missing peripherals, delivery and applicable tax in separate fields. A headline amount can be a reasonable starting point, but it is not your delivered cost. Do not copy a US price into the CAD column or treat a currency conversion as a Canadian offer. A local seller, regional warranty and actual stock still need verification.

Sources: [Intel — How to build a gaming PC](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/gaming/resources/how-to-build-a-gaming-pc.html)

### Give every candidate an exact identity

Record the retailer URL, seller name, manufacturer model and exact configuration or SKU. Keep a saved description of the CPU, graphics card, installed memory, storage and operating system. Two machines with the same family name may differ in cooling, power supply or memory arrangement. A listing that promises a component family rather than the installed model leaves an important question open. Ask the seller to identify it in writing before treating the row as complete.

For memory, record capacity and module arrangement separately. For storage, distinguish the installed drive from an empty expansion position. For networking, note whether the advertised connection is included hardware or an optional upgrade. Treat omissions as unknown fields. Unknown is a reason to investigate; it does not mean that the component is poor, free or compatible with your next upgrade.

A practical US offer checklist for exact desktop SKUs, marketplace sellers, return terms and benchmark context.

### Make the offer, not the advertisement, your comparison unit

An advertisement can describe a product family while the offer you can buy is one configuration from one seller. Open the product page and select the intended variant before copying anything. Record the exact model or SKU, CPU, GPU, installed RAM and storage. Include the seller and condition in the same row. A new desktop from the manufacturer and a refurbished example from another merchant are different offers even when their specifications appear similar.

Keep all amounts in USD and separate the advertised price from delivery, tax and extras. Do not turn an expired coupon into the default price. If a discount needs membership, financing, a trade-in or another purchase, record that condition explicitly. A useful comparison lets another person understand which amount they could reproduce and when it was seen. It does not imply that the offer is the cheapest anywhere.

### Check the components a headline omits

A CPU and GPU pair is an incomplete description of a desktop. Ask for the installed power supply, motherboard, cooler, memory module arrangement and case. A seller may permit component substitutions, so distinguish an exact promise from an example photograph. If the listing does not identify the part, keep that field unknown rather than filling it from a similar machine or a third-party review.

Make a separate list of your physical requirements: monitor outputs, wireless networking, audio connection, front ports and space around the enclosure. Count what you need now, then record which future changes matter to you. Upgrading a drive and replacing a graphics card require different information. The complete-system planning checklist is useful here because it makes less visible parts of the purchase explicit.

## C03 — Build vs Buy: compare the complete PC cost

Put a DIY build and a prebuilt on the same cost basis, including Windows, missing parts, support and your time.

### Compare two systems that solve the same job

Begin with a workload and a display target, then write down two complete routes to that result. One route is the exact prebuilt configuration you can order. The other is a compatible DIY parts list. Matching only the CPU and graphics processor leaves room for large differences in memory, storage, cooling, networking or service. Decide which differences you accept and record them before comparing totals.

A complete DIY plan includes the motherboard, power supply, case and cooling as well as the most visible components. Add operating-system requirements, missing cables, wireless hardware and peripherals when your situation needs them. Components you already own can reduce the new cash outlay, but list them and check their condition and compatibility. Treating an unverified old part as a free solution can make a comparison look settled too early.

Sources: [Intel — How to build a gaming PC](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/gaming/resources/how-to-build-a-gaming-pc.html)

### Separate observed cash costs from personal assumptions

Create one row for each cost and record its source. Keep a retailer-observed amount separate from your estimate of shipping or assembly time. Add tax consistently for both routes when it is known, and show it as unknown when it is not. Use the same region and currency. A converted foreign price is a planning assumption, not a domestic offer that you can buy on those terms.

For each exact new part or system, save the seller, condition, URL and observation time. A subtotal assembled from different days can be useful for planning, but it is not a guaranteed checkout total. Refresh stale rows together before making the purchase. Do not label the resulting difference as savings unless both routes remain available, comparable and complete when checked.

*Use the same cost basis for both routes*

| Category | DIY route | Prebuilt route |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hardware | Every required new component | Exact selected system configuration |
| Software | Actual licence or verified existing entitlement | Included edition and licence description |
| Fulfilment | Delivery for all separate orders | System delivery and required extras |
| Service | Your chosen repair and support plan | Actual seller/manufacturer warranty terms |
| Time | A personal planning input | A personal planning input |

### Do not assume that Windows costs nothing

Record the operating-system edition and the licence basis for each route. An included prebuilt licence is part of that configuration; an existing licence on another machine may have conditions. Microsoft notes that a significant hardware change such as a motherboard replacement can require Windows reactivation. Matching the installed edition also matters. Those facts make software a checkable row rather than a casual zero.

Linking an account or retaining a product key does not, by itself, settle whether a licence can be transferred. Check the terms attached to your actual licence or ask the relevant provider. If that question remains open, leave the DIY software cost unresolved and compare a second scenario with a new licence. Avoid choosing a dubious key offer simply to make one route look cheaper.

Sources: [Microsoft Support — Reactivating Windows after a hardware change](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/activation/reactivating-windows-after-a-hardware-change)

### Value support and time explicitly

A prebuilt can place more of the diagnosis and system repair behind one support route. A DIY build gives you control over the parts and assembly, but failures may involve different vendors. Neither description guarantees a pleasant repair. Read the actual terms and think about what happens if the machine stops working during an important week. Return shipping, backup equipment and your willingness to troubleshoot affect the decision.

If you assign money to your time, label it as your own assumption. Assembly, software setup, research and fault finding are separate activities, and beginners may value the learning itself. Run the comparison with and without a time allowance so that the cash difference stays visible. A personal hourly value is not a market measurement and should not be presented as a universal DIY labour charge.
